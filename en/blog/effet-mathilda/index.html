The Matilda Effect

In this article
  1. From the Matthew effect to the Matilda effect
  2. A few examples
  3. Today
  4. Sources

The Matilda effect is the recurrent and systemic denial, misappropriation or downplaying of women's contributions to scientific research. Their work is thus very often attributed to their male colleagues.

The phenomenon was first described by the suffragist and abolitionist Matilda Joslyn Gage (1826–1898) in her essay Woman as an Inventor (first published as a pamphlet in 1870).

In it, she notes that women's traces have been erased from the history of inventions, evidently to the benefit of men. Inventiveness is closely tied to the freedom of the people.

And although women are in fact responsible for a great many inventions, they are far less free than their male counterparts in exercising their intellectual power and protecting their ideas.

“Deprived as she is of political power, woman must face contempt for her sex, the ostentatious or concealed disdain for the female condition, belittling allusions to her intellectual abilities, so many elements that tend to hinder the expression of her creative genius.”

Matilda Joslyn Gage (translated from English)

From the Matthew effect to the Matilda effect

In 1993, Margaret W. Rossiter, a historian of science, took up the Matthew effect and theorised the Matilda effect. The Matthew effect, proposed by Robert K. Merton in 1968, refers to a sentence from the Gospel of Matthew:

“For to everyone who has, more will be given, and he will have an abundance; but from the one who has not, even what he has will be taken away”

It is notably used to theorise the idea that the most recognised scientists tend to maintain dominance in research, to the detriment of their colleagues.

Rossiter observes a similar effect, even amplified, in the systematic discrimination against women in science and in the recognition of their work.

Thus the Matilda effect aims to make women invisible and to erase them. It can take the form of:

  • the appropriation of women's ideas by men
  • the downplaying of women's participation in scientific research and discoveries
  • women's names not being retained in scientific discoveries

Some examples of Matilda effects (Quai des savoirs)

Chien-Shiung Wu
Proves major theories in quantum mechanics, but it is her colleagues Yang and Lee who receive the 1957 Nobel Prize in Physics, without her.
Marie Tharp
Discovers in 1952 that parts of the Earth's crust drift, which the geologist Bruce C. Heezen considered “scientific heresy” and a “girl thing”.
Rosalind Franklin
Produces several X-ray images of DNA that are shown without her knowledge by the physicist Maurice Wilkins. He receives the 1962 Nobel Prize in Medicine for this discovery while Rosalind Franklin is barely mentioned

Today

Progress is being made, thanks to the efforts of committed associations and organisations. But we are still far from the goal.

Every year the International Day of Women and Girls in Science is celebrated on 11 February, since its proclamation by the United Nations General Assembly in 2015.

It honours the remarkable contributions of women in science and raises awareness of the importance of advancing gender equality in scientific fields in order to meet the planetary challenges ahead.
